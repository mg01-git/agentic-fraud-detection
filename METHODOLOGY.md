# Methodology: How We Got to This Dataset

This is the "how and why" story behind the dataset, kept separate from `FINDINGS.md` (results/metrics) and `OPEN_ITEMS_FOR_LATER.md` (deferred work), so there's one place explaining the reasoning end to end without having to dig through chat history.

## Step 1: Started with a real downloaded dataset — and rejected it

We started with a real-looking fraud detection dataset found online (Kaggle-style; saved locally as `Fraud Detection Dataset.csv`), with columns very similar to what we ended up with: transaction amount, type, device, location, prior fraud count, account age, transaction velocity, payment method, and a binary fraud label. 51,000 rows, ~4.9% fraud rate.

**We rejected it after diagnosing two serious problems:**

1. **No real separation between fraud and legitimate transactions on any feature.** We compared averages for fraud vs. legit across every numeric column (amount, prior fraud count, account age, velocity, hour of day) and every categorical column (transaction type, device, location, payment method) — every single one showed fraud and legit within about 1 percentage point of each other, or statistically indistinguishable averages. This meant the label appeared to be generated close to randomly, disconnected from the other columns — not usable for building a system meant to demonstrate real risk reasoning.

2. **Logically impossible combinations.** We cross-tabbed Transaction_Type against Payment_Method and found combinations that can't happen in reality — e.g. "ATM Withdrawal" paired with "Net Banking" thousands of times, which makes no sense (you can't withdraw physical cash via an internet bank transfer). Every type/method combination appeared roughly equally often, meaning the columns were generated independently of each other with no underlying logic connecting them.

**Conclusion:** the original dataset was very likely generated by drawing each column independently at random, with no causal generating process tying transaction type, payment method, features, and the fraud label together. Not fixable by cleaning — the label itself carried no real signal. This diagnosis (checking for feature/label separation and cross-column logical consistency) is itself a reusable technique worth naming in an interview, independent of this specific dataset.

## Step 2: Decision to build a synthetic dataset instead — and resolving the "circularity" concern

Once we decided to generate our own data, an important objection came up: if we also write the rule that generates the fraud labels, doesn't that make any later "detection" circular — aren't we just testing whether the system can find a rule we handed it?

**Resolution:** the label-generating logic (in `generate_synthetic_data.py`) is kept strictly separate from anything the Decision Agent or the baseline model ever sees. The dataset itself (`paypal_transactions.csv`) contains only raw transaction fields — no column reveals which archetype (if any) produced a row, or what the true fraud probability was. That information lives only in `ground_truth_HIDDEN.csv`, used solely for our own evaluation, never fed to the agent or the model as input. Both the baseline model and the future Decision Agent have to find/reason about the pattern from the raw data itself — exactly as they would with real transaction data. This is standard practice for synthetic benchmarks in ML research: you always know the ground truth because you generated it, but the system under test never sees the generating process, only its output.

## Step 3: Choosing the business domain

We initially considered modeling this as a traditional bank (like Chase) monitoring a customer's checking account plus a separately-issued credit card — but this got confusing fast, particularly around what "payment method = Credit Card" even means when there's no explicit `Account_Type` column distinguishing which product a transaction belongs to (a real, documented schema gap in the original downloaded dataset too).

**We ultimately chose a PayPal-style digital wallet instead**, based on Mansi's own past experience with PayPal's business, because: it's architecturally simpler (one account per user — a balance plus a linked funding source — no multi-account confusion), it's a domain both familiar and quickly explainable to any interviewer, and the two fraud archetypes Mansi proposed (account takeover, stolen funding instrument) mapped directly onto well-known real PayPal fraud patterns without inventing anything.

## Step 4: Designing and iterating the two fraud archetypes

Two archetypes were chosen deliberately over a single generic "weighted risk formula," because a single formula tends to make every fraud case look the same — boring for an explainability demo, and not realistic (real fraud has distinct patterns, not one flavor).

**Account takeover** — iterated through several review rounds to add: device/IP-location mismatch from the user's normal pattern, a brand-new recipient or a newly-linked destination bank (`Withdrawal_Destination_Bank_Age_Days`), higher amounts (draining behavior), unusual hours, and — a signal Mansi specifically added — a bias toward **dormant accounts** (`Days_Since_Last_Activity`), since real account-takeover targets are disproportionately accounts nobody's been actively watching.

**Stolen funding instrument** — iterated to add: a recently-linked funding instrument (`Funding_Instrument_Age_Days`, kept as a *separate* field from the withdrawal-destination-age field after Mansi correctly pointed out they represent opposite directions of money flow and shouldn't be merged), high-resale-value purchase categories (`Purchase_Category` — Electronics, Gift Cards, Travel), elevated transaction velocity, and — after a detailed discussion of how card-issuer authorization actually works (verification at card-linking vs. decline at transaction-authorization time) — a realistic **decline/card-testing burst pattern** (`Transaction_Status`, `Decline_Rate_Last_24H`), where several rapid attempts get declined by the issuer before, occasionally, one succeeds.

Explicitly decided against modeling instrument-level fraud history across the whole PayPal ecosystem (tracking a stolen card across *other* accounts, not just this one) — a real, stronger production signal, but out of scope for now; documented in `OPEN_ITEMS_FOR_LATER.md`.

Explicitly decided fraud history (`Previous_Fraudulent_Transactions`) stays at the **account level**, and that a chargeback is treated as folded into "confirmed fraud" rather than tracked as its own separate field — kept simple on purpose.

Neither archetype was built to be perfectly separable — both have noise layered on top, and about 30% of all fraud rows have no archetype at all (pure unexplained/noise fraud), so a system that scored close to 100% would itself be a red flag, same as in the real world.

## Step 5: Validation and bugs found along the way

Every design decision was checked against the actual generated data, not just assumed to work:

- Cross-tab checks (Transaction_Type × Funding_Source, Transaction_Type × Counterparty_ID, presence rules for archetype-specific fields) — found and fixed a real bug where account-takeover withdrawals sometimes kept a stale recipient ID instead of "SELF-BANK."
- Signal-strength checks confirmed the intended patterns actually show up strongly in the data (e.g. ~99.5–99.7% device/location mismatch on account-takeover rows vs. ~0% normal; ~58% decline rate on stolen-instrument rows vs. ~4% normal).
- Ground-truth factor-list accuracy was checked programmatically — found and fixed two bugs where contributing factors ("dormant_account_reactivated," "high_24h_velocity") were being listed even when the underlying condition wasn't actually true for that row, which would have quietly broken the later faithfulness eval.
- A subtle tie-breaking bug affecting 22 of 4,000 users' `Days_Since_Last_Activity` was traced to card-testing burst follow-ups occasionally landing on the exact same hour as their seed transaction — fixed by guaranteeing follow-ups always land strictly later.
- `Days_Since_Last_Activity` for each user's first observed transaction is drawn from a real distribution (not defaulted to zero or artificially floored at 1) — a value of 0 is legitimate and occasionally occurs, matching the math.

## Step 6: Device ID redesign (v3) — and a second bug found while making the change

While reviewing `lookup_user_history`'s design, Mansi correctly identified that "device mismatch" as originally built (Mobile/Desktop/Tablet category) was a weak, noisy signal — real users routinely and legitimately switch between their own devices of different types, so a category change alone shouldn't read as suspicious. The fix: each user now owns a small set of real devices (usually 1–3), each with a unique `Device_ID` plus its own type. Normal transactions randomly draw from the user's own known devices (so type can vary transaction to transaction with zero mismatch signal). Account-takeover transactions use a fresh `Device_ID` never before seen on that account — regardless of whether its type happens to coincidentally match one of the user's own. This is the real signal: an unrecognized device, not a changed category.

While making this change, a second, unrelated bug was found and fixed: card-testing burst follow-up transactions (the 1–3 rapid attempts after a stolen-funding-instrument "seed" transaction) were not actually being labeled as `stolen_funding_instrument` in the ground truth, or getting the archetype's elevated decline probability — they were silently falling back to independent random/baseline labeling. This meant a meaningful chunk of what should have been archetype-driven data was miscategorized as unexplained noise. Fixed by forcing follow-up rows to inherit their seed transaction's archetype treatment. This changed the numbers materially: stolen-funding-instrument archetype rows went from 1,047 to 3,090, and overall fraud rate rose from ~5% to ~7.5% (see FINDINGS.md for updated model results).

Also fixed in this same pass: `train_baseline_model.py` was discovered to still be using its own separate, older inline feature-engineering function instead of the shared `features.py` module — meaning the two had already started to drift apart without anyone noticing. Consolidated onto `features.py` as the single source of truth for both training and (later) live inference.

## Step 7: Baseline model as a further validation check

Training a real predictive model on the final dataset (Phase 2, see `FINDINGS.md`) served a dual purpose: producing the `get_risk_score` tool the agent will use, and acting as a final sanity check on the whole dataset design. The model's own top features, discovered statistically with no hints from us, lined up exactly with what we deliberately engineered (Device/Location_Mismatch, Decline_Rate_Last_24H, velocity, funding instrument age) — confirming the archetypes are real, learnable structure, not accidental noise.

## Step 8: Enrichment tools (device/email), a real gray zone for stolen_funding_instrument, and reframing "noise fraud" as friendly fraud

Once the risk-score distribution was actually inspected (see FINDINGS.md), a real design gap showed up: the score is strongly **bimodal** — ~89% of transactions cluster low (fraud rate ~1.5-1.8%), ~10% cluster high (fraud rate ~60-65%), and almost nothing sits literally in between. Within that high-score cluster, `account_takeover` had a natural source of ambiguity already (its dormancy boost is partial, not all-or-nothing), but `stolen_funding_instrument` did not — its signals (new funding instrument, high-risk category, high decline rate, elevated velocity) fired as a single all-or-nothing bundle, so every SFI-pattern case looked equally suspicious to the risk model with no way to tell the true fraud attempts apart from the ones that would resolve as legitimate.

**The fix, and why it maps one-to-one to the two archetypes:** two new reference tables, kept deliberately SEPARATE from `paypal_transactions.csv` (not merged in as free columns) to represent real third-party data a fraud system would query selectively, not something baked into every row:

- `device_threat_intel.csv` (keyed by `Device_ID`): `Is_Jailbroken`, `Device_Threat_Rating`. Wired to `account_takeover` — the attacker's unrecognized device gets an elevated (~40%, not 100%) jailbroken rate vs. a ~4% baseline for a user's own known devices (real users occasionally jailbreak their own phones for non-fraud reasons, so this stays probabilistic).
- `email_risk_data.csv` (keyed by `Email`): `Email_Age_Days`, `Email_Risk_Rating`. Wired to `stolen_funding_instrument` — a newer/higher-risk email (<90 days old) partially skews an account toward SFI-pattern attempts in the first place, and independently nudges those attempts toward actually being confirmed fraud. Only the `Email` ID itself lives in the main transaction file (matching how `Device_ID` already worked) — age and risk rating live only in the reference table, queried on demand.

Both nudges are **partial correlations, not rules** — added directly into `fraud_prob` alongside the archetype's other factors, the same pattern already used for dormancy in `account_takeover`. This was checked, not assumed: after regenerating, within the risk model's own high-score (>0.85) band, jailbroken-device account_takeover cases are meaningfully more likely to be confirmed fraud than non-jailbroken ones (43.9% vs. 29.4%), and new-email stolen_funding_instrument cases are meaningfully more likely to be confirmed fraud than established-email ones (16.5% vs. 7.9%) — real, if imperfect, separation the risk model alone cannot see, because Email and device-threat data live outside its feature set on purpose. This is the concrete answer to "why would the agent need to call more tools instead of just trusting the score" — the score genuinely cannot resolve these cases; the enrichment tools can partially help, and the agent has to reason about a signal that's a real corroborator, not a giveaway (an important, deliberately-preserved subtlety — see the "gray-zone ATO" honest edge case in `DEMO_SET.md` where a jailbroken device still turned out to be legitimate).

## Step 9: `lookup_user_history` and `policy_lookup` — why separate tools, not more model features

Two more tools were designed before touching the agent itself, prompted by the question "why isn't this just part of the risk score?"

**`policy_lookup`** is deliberately narrow: a single mandatory rule (`Transaction_Amount >= $500` triggers mandatory escalation, read from `POLICY.md`), chosen specifically because it's independent of every other signal in the system — it fires on ordinary legitimate large purchases just as often as on fraud (verified: 2.0% of transactions trigger it, 31% of those are confirmed fraud). Its value isn't demonstrating tool-calling (the enrichment tools already do that more realistically) — it's demonstrating a genuinely different *kind* of evidence: a categorical business rule that applies regardless of fraud likelihood, alongside probabilistic evidence the agent has to weigh. A restricted-purchase-category version of this rule was considered and dropped — a truly banned category would just be a checkout-time decline in reality, not an ambiguous case worth an agent's judgment.

**`lookup_user_history`** exists separately from the risk model's own device/location features for a reason beyond just "the agent needs something to investigate": the model's `Device_Mismatch`/`Location_Mismatch` are computed once, offline, from each user's *full* history (a documented, accepted training-time simplification) and frozen into `risk_model.pkl`. A live tool computing the same comparison correctly can only look at what happened *before* the transaction being evaluated — which is what a real production system would actually need, not an optional nicety. It's also the raw, citable evidence behind the agent's explanation: "risk score 0.93" isn't independently checkable; "this device has never appeared in this account's prior 14 transactions" is.

Along the way, redesigned what "known" means for a device/location: not frequency-based ("seen more than once," the model's definition), but tenure-based — first seen at least 30 days before the current transaction. This closes a real gap: a frequency rule can be gamed *within* a single attack (an attacker reusing the same new device several times in one day could make it "known" to later transactions in the same burst); tenure can't be faked within one session. An early version of this also permanently disqualified a device the moment any transaction on it was ever confirmed fraudulent — found and reverted after testing against real data: `stolen_funding_instrument` fraud, by design, happens on the account's own legitimate device (only the funding instrument is stolen), so that rule was permanently blacklisting a genuine user's own phone after one unrelated fraud dispute. Tenure alone already closes the poisoning gap it was meant to prevent, so the extra rule added no protection and caused real harm — confirmed on a specific user (33 clean transactions across 171 days, incorrectly shown as having zero recognized devices under the old rule). The tool also returns an explicit thin-history flag (too few transactions and/or too short an observed time span) so the agent knows to discount "typical for this user" claims on newer accounts, and live-counted 24h transaction/decline counts as an honest cross-check against the main file's generated (not literally aggregated) versions of those fields.

**Reframing "noise fraud" as friendly fraud:** the ~30% of confirmed fraud rows that match neither archetype (`True_Archetype = "none"`, `Fraudulent = 1`) were originally documented only abstractly as "unexplained fraud." Mansi's suggestion to name this concretely as **friendly fraud** (a customer disputes a transaction that was, at the time, completely normal-looking — no device, location, velocity, or instrument anomaly to find) is a documentation-only reframe, not a data change: it already mechanically represents exactly this concept, since it's fraud confirmed after the fact with zero transaction-time signal by construction. This gives the interview story a concrete, defensible real-world category instead of an abstract "noise" placeholder, and motivates a specific, honest thing the Decision Agent should say when it can't find a pattern: not "this is safe," but "no transaction-time red flags found — if disputed, this may be a case with no detectable signal (e.g. a friendly-fraud claim) rather than a missed pattern."
